    Why one chat window does not fit everyone

    Think about the last time you phoned a supplier. "Press 1 for invoices, 2 for deliveries, 3 for something else." You had two problems and the menu had room for one, so you picked the wrong branch, waited, and explained everything twice. Your colleague, who only ever calls about deliveries, finds the same menu perfectly fine. That difference is the point. The MIT course this module borrows from, Designing and Building AI Products and Services, puts it plainly in its module on human-computer interaction: every user is different, so a one-size-fits-all interface does not work, and that gap is exactly where AI earns its place in an interface.

    Here is what that means for a chat front door. A menu is the system's view of the world: its departments, its database tables, its five allowed reasons for calling. An intention-first interface starts from what the person is trying to do, in their own words, and then works out what the system needs. Read the messages on the left. "Charged twice and I also need to change the billing address" is two intentions and one missing fact. The menu forces a choice and quietly drops the second need. The agent keeps both and asks one question.

    I do not want to oversell this. Menus are predictable, cheap and testable, and they never misunderstand. Free text can be misread, and the MIT lecturer's own example is the worse kind of failure: a voice assistant that confidently started an alarm instead of adding an item to a shopping list. Doing the wrong thing with confidence is worse than admitting you did not understand. So the design rule for this week is a balance: start from the person's intention, then put a scripted step wherever a mistake is expensive and the right answer can be checked. Collecting an email address is scripted. Working out what an upset customer actually needs is not.

    Four habits from the same course. The MIT workbook for that module asks you to check an interface against usability heuristics. Four of them run through this whole week: visibility of system status (Day 2, streaming), recognition rather than recall (suggested prompts instead of a blank box), user control and freedom (Day 3, the handover to a person), and error prevention (the validation loops you meet next).

    Design around the person's intention, not the system's menu. Script only the steps where a mistake is expensive and the answer is checkable.

    Scripted blocks, variables and loops: the three builders

    Every onboarding form you have ever filled in does something quietly clever: it refuses an email address without an "@" and asks again. Nobody calls that artificial intelligence. It is a rule, a stored value and a loop, and a conversation designer draws exactly the same thing as blocks on a canvas. The syllabus calls this block-based dialogue with state management, loops and variable handling. The flow on the left is one.

    There are three common ways to build one. n8n's own Chat Trigger gives you a hosted chat page in one click and no blocks at all: the agent handles everything, which is enough for most internal tools and is what Lab 5.1 uses. Typebot is an open-source builder where you drag text, input and condition blocks, store answers in variables such as {{email}}, and call your n8n webhook from a block. Voiceflow is the richer commercial cousin, with intents, entities and a knowledge base, and an API step that can call the same n8n agent. The products differ; the vocabulary is shared. A block is one step. A variable is a named slot that keeps its value for the whole conversation. A condition branches. A loop repeats a block until the input passes a check. A handoff passes the collected state to the agent.

    Press Play and keep one eye on the variables panel. Priya types "priya.example.com", the email block loops, and the slot stays empty until her second try. The intent block branches to invoices, the next block fills invoice_id, and the final block hands the whole state to the n8n agent, which does the open-ended work: finding the invoice, seeing the duplicate payment, offering the refund. State management sounds grand; it is that panel. Which slots are filled, which are empty, and what to do about the empty ones.

    Where this breaks: people do not follow scripts. They answer two questions at once, or say "actually, forget the invoice, it is about the delivery". A long block flow is a phone tree with extra steps, and customers can feel it. So keep the scripted part short, two or three facts the agent genuinely cannot work without, each validated, and hand off early. Everything after the handoff is Week 7's agent and Week 8's tools.

    Script the predictable: a few facts, validated, stored in variables. Hand everything else to the agent, early.

    Streaming, and a memory that lasts until tomorrow

    Streaming and the shape of a wait

    You message a colleague a question and the little "typing" indicator appears at once; you relax, because something is happening. Another colleague goes silent for six seconds and then drops a whole paragraph on you. Same answer, same six seconds, very different experience. The MIT workbook for the human-computer interaction module gives this a ladder: under 0.1 seconds feels immediate, 0.1 to 1 second is a stammer, 1 to 10 seconds is an interruption, and beyond 10 seconds is a disruption. An agent that looks up two invoices and writes 150 tokens takes about six seconds end to end. On the ladder that is an interruption, and interruptions are where people open another tab.

    Streaming is the fix, and it costs nothing. In Week 1 you watched Claude produce a reply one token at a time; the API can send each token the moment it exists instead of waiting for the last one. In n8n, the Chat Trigger has a Response Mode setting: switch it to Streaming and the first words appear within a fraction of a second. Watch the left window on the canvas. Nothing got faster. The first word simply arrived at 0.3 seconds instead of 6, which moves the wait two rungs down the ladder and satisfies the workbook's first heuristic, visibility of system status. The person can see the system working.

    Two places where streaming does not help. Tool calls happen before the first token: the CRM lookup takes a second or two and there is nothing to stream, so show a status line ("looking up your invoices…") rather than silence. And WhatsApp cannot stream at all; messages arrive whole, which is one reason replies on that channel should be short. Try the long reply with streaming off and read the ladder.

    The canvas is slowed down. A real model writes 40 to 60 words a second, so a typical reply types out in two or three seconds; most of the six seconds are the lookup and the model's first-token latency. I paced the words to be watchable. The shape of the comparison is honest; the speed is not.

    Streaming does not make the agent faster. It makes the wait visible, and a visible wait feels like a fraction of a silent one.

    Session ids, and what the assistant should remember

    You know the bad version of this from any support desk: the call drops, you ring back, and the second person asks you to start from the beginning. The good version has a ticket number; quote it and the context comes with you. In a chat front end the ticket number is the session id, a short string the front end sends with every message. The memory node reads and writes under that key and nothing else. The n8n chat widget invents one per browser. WhatsApp hands you the sender's phone number. A customer portal where people sign in can pass the customer's account id, so memory follows the account rather than the browser.

    Run the test on the left. Browser A says her name; browser B asks "what is my name?". With two ids, B is told, correctly, that the assistant does not know yet. Tick "same session ID" and run it again: B is told Priya's name. That is a privacy leak and a wrong answer in one move, and the only thing that changed was a string. The session id is the whole of "whose memory is this".

    The memory type decides how long it lasts. The Window Buffer memory lives inside n8n's own process and keeps the last few turns; a restart or a redeploy empties it. Postgres or Redis chat memory writes each turn as a row in a database, survives restarts, and lets the Chat Trigger's "Load previous session" option reload the history when a returning visitor opens a new tab. Press "Come back tomorrow" with each type and watch the difference.

    Then the question the syllabus asks and most teams skip: what to store, and what to forget. Store what helps next time: a name, an organisation, a preferred language, the open consignment or invoice ids, a one-paragraph summary of the last conversation. Never store passwords, one-time codes, full card or bank numbers, or anything the person asked you to forget. Tick "redact sensitive fields" and run the test again: a step between the trigger and the memory replaces the card number before it is written. And give memory an expiry. A support history that outlives its purpose is a liability under most data-protection rules, GDPR's storage limitation among them, and under your own company's retention policy.

    n8n will not do the forgetting for you. Redaction is a Set or Code node you add; expiry is a rule in your database or a scheduled workflow that deletes old rows. Write both into the design before Lab 5.1, not after the first complaint.

    The session id decides whose memory the agent reads. The memory type decides how long it lasts. You decide what never goes in.

    Knowing when to stop: handover and identity

    An assistant on a public channel needs two reflexes a demo never needed. It has to know when to bring in a person, and it has to know who it is talking to before it changes anything that matters. By the end you will have written both reflexes into your agent's system prompt and watched the rules fire in a simulation.

    Pick a conversation and press Play. Rules are checked after every customer message.

    Handing over to a person, and taking the context along

    The best receptionist I ever worked with had one sentence ready: "Let me get you someone who can actually help with that." Then she walked over and briefed them, so you never said anything twice. That is a handover, and the MIT workbook for the human-computer interaction module has a heuristic for it: user control and freedom, the clearly marked emergency exit. A chat window with no route to a person is a trap with a friendly face. People notice, and they screenshot it.

    When should the exit open? Five triggers cover most of it, and you can switch each one on and off on the left. The customer asks for a person. The agent has failed twice in a row. Money or a commitment above a threshold is on the table. The message carries anger or distress. The request is out of scope: legal, HR, medical, press. The rules are evaluated after every customer turn, which is the mechanism to copy. In n8n the first four live in the system prompt, with the agent returning a small structured flag through the output parser you met in Week 7. The money rule also lives in an IF node that checks the number itself, because a threshold is too important to leave to prose.

    How you hand over matters as much as when. The agent composes a packet: a one-line summary, who the customer is and which channel they came through, the session id, the intentions found, the amount at stake, what the agent has already promised, and a link to the transcript. That goes to a queue your team watches, a Gmail alert or a Slack message or a ticket in the intake workflow from Week 6. The customer is told once, in plain words, that a colleague is taking over and that nothing needs repeating. And the agent stops answering on that session, a flag in memory, until a person closes it.

    Two ways this goes wrong. Hand over too eagerly and you have built a contact form with extra steps; hand over too late and you have built the screenshots. Try the contract conversation with "out of scope" and "two failures" switched off: the customer loops, and the agent never notices. Measure your handover rate and the reasons behind it; after Week 1's failure notebook, that log is the most useful evaluation data you will own.

    Decide the handover triggers before launch, check them after every turn, and send the context with the customer so nobody repeats themselves.

    Verifying identity before the agent acts

    Every finance team has seen the email: "We have changed banks; please pay future invoices to this account." The good teams ring the supplier back on the number they already had before changing anything, because the email proves nothing about who wrote it. Hold the assistant to the same rule, and then some. Over a public channel a message proves nothing about who typed it. A website widget is anonymous. A WhatsApp number tells you which phone sent the message, not which person is holding it; phones are lost, SIMs are swapped, and staff leave with the company handset.

    The mechanism is a ladder, and the gate on the left walks it. Classify each request by what it would change. Reading something that is safe to show anyone who knows the reference, such as where consignment MR-48812 is, needs no check at all. Changing something about an account, like a delivery address, needs one proof: something only the customer knows or receives, typically a six-digit code sent to the email already on file. Moving money or changing where money goes needs two proofs and a person's approval; the assistant prepares the request and never executes it. In n8n this is an IF node on an action_level the agent returns, with the sensitive branch sending a code through your Gmail node and a Wait node holding the conversation until it comes back, or simply opening a ticket for a colleague.

    The MIT course makes the wider point in its module on GANs and deepfakes: a convincing fake face or voice is now cheap to make, and the lecturer's honest conclusion is that detecting them is an arms race with no winner in sight. So the wording of a request, its tone, even a voice note in the right accent, can never be your evidence. Only something the channel cannot fake can: a code delivered to a contact you already trust, a login to the portal, a callback to a known number.

    Do not over-gate. Asking for a code before telling someone when their parcel arrives is how you lose users in a week. The ladder exists so that most requests pass with no friction at all, and only the few that could cost money or expose data slow down.

    Verify in proportion to what the action could cost. The channel tells you which device is talking; it never tells you who.

    Embedding on a web page, and the two locks on a public door

    Look at your own company's website and the odds are there is a round button in the bottom-right corner. Look at the HR page on the intranet and there may be a chat box sitting inside the page itself. Those are the two embeds, and both are one paste away. An iframe drops n8n's whole hosted chat page into yours in one tag: isolated, quick, and it looks like n8n's page rather than yours. The floating bubble is a small script tag that loads n8n's own chat widget, @n8n/chat, which talks straight to the Chat Trigger's webhook and takes your colours through CSS variables. Both need two things from Week 6: the workflow must be Active, and the address must be the production URL, not the test URL that only works while you watch in the editor.

    Now the uncomfortable part. That webhook URL is public. Anyone who reads your page's source can see it, and every message to it costs you Claude tokens. The first lock is allowed origins. A browser sends an Origin header with every request, the Chat Trigger has an Allowed Origins option, and a page served from any other host is refused before the agent runs. Send a request from evil.example on the left and watch it bounce. Be honest about what this lock does: it stops other people's web pages, because browsers enforce it. It does not stop a script or a command line, which can send any header they like. It is a lock on the front door, not the walls.

    The walls are a rate limit and a spending cap. Fire the burst on the left: the eleventh request in a minute gets a 429 and a polite "let's slow down", and the agent never runs. n8n Cloud does not ship a rate limiter on the Chat Trigger, so you build one: a Code node that counts messages per session id in the workflow's static data, or a gateway such as Cloudflare in front of n8n. Behind that sits the spending limit you set in the Anthropic Console in Week 8, which is the lock that works even when everything else fails. And watch your execution count after launch; a quiet spike at 3 am is how abuse usually announces itself.

    Abuse is not only volume. Someone will try to use your free assistant as a general-purpose chatbot, and someone will paste a prompt injection into it, the attack you learned to quarantine in Week 2. A system prompt that says plainly what the assistant is for, and declines the rest in one friendly sentence, handles most of both.

    The webhook is public. Allowed origins lock the front door, a rate limit and a spending cap are the walls, and the system prompt decides what the assistant will even talk about.

    WhatsApp through the Cloud API

    Drivers, warehouse supervisors and small customers do not live in your portal. They live in WhatsApp, and a channel that meets people where they already are gets used without a launch campaign. There are two routes in. Meta's WhatsApp Cloud API is the direct one: a developer account gives you a free test number that can message up to five phone numbers you verify, and later a business verification for a real number. Wati is a vendor that wraps the same API in a dashboard with a trial: easier to set up, a monthly fee, and your n8n workflow sees a webhook either way.

    Press Replay and follow the dot. Priya sends a message; Meta posts it to your n8n WhatsApp Trigger; the same agent you built in Week 7 runs, with memory keyed on her phone number; and n8n calls the send-message endpoint to deliver the reply. Before any of that works there is a one-time handshake: when you save the webhook URL, Meta calls it with a challenge value and expects the value echoed back. The n8n WhatsApp Trigger answers that for you, which is the one piece of plumbing people get stuck on when they build it by hand.

    Three differences from the web widget. There is no streaming: messages arrive whole, so keep replies short and send "let me look that up" first when a tool call is slow. There is a 24-hour customer-service window: you may reply freely within 24 hours of the person's last message, and outside it only with a pre-approved template message, which matters for any proactive alert your agent sends. And, from Day 3, the number is known but the person is not, so the identity ladder applies exactly as it did on the website. One advantage runs the other way: WhatsApp's interactive messages give you buttons and lists, which is the MIT workbook's recognition-over-recall heuristic in a channel people already know.

    Safety in the sandbox. The test number messages only phones on its recipient list, so you will be adding your own. Use made-up customers, and blur every phone number in the screenshots you hand in.

    One agent, three front doors: the channel router

    Think about how a good support team works. Email, phone and the web form all land in one queue, with one set of rules and one history per customer. Nobody builds three teams. The same discipline applies here, and it is the architecture your capstone in Week 10 will stand on: one AI Agent node, one system prompt, one memory store, one set of tools, and a thin layer in front that differs per channel. Click the three channels on the left and watch what changes and what does not.

    What changes is small and specific. The trigger node: a Chat Trigger for the widget and the iframe, a WhatsApp Trigger for the phone. The session key: the widget's generated id, the portal's customer id, the sender's phone number. The reply path: streamed back over the open connection for the web, a send-message call for WhatsApp. And the identity baseline from Day 3: anonymous on the public widget, signed in on the portal, number known but person unknown on WhatsApp. Everything in the middle is shared, so a fix to the system prompt or a new tool reaches every channel at once, and the memory row for a customer who writes on the website in the morning and on WhatsApp in the afternoon can be the same row if you map both ids to the same account.

    Where it gets awkward: the channels do not agree on format. A streamed, 120-word reply with a markdown table reads well in the widget and arrives as a wall of asterisks on WhatsApp. The router is where you fix that, with a channel field passed into the system prompt ("you are answering on WhatsApp: plain text, under 60 words, one question at a time") and a Set node that strips formatting on the way out. Build the router once, in Lab 5.2, and every channel you add after it is a day's work, not a project.

    Vary only the front: trigger, session key, reply path, identity baseline. Share everything behind it, and tell the agent which channel it is speaking on.

    1. Trigger. Duplicate the Week 7 support agent. Replace its trigger with a Chat Trigger set to Public, Response Mode Streaming, and "Load previous session" enabled.

      Where the settings live

      Chat Trigger → "Make Chat Publicly Available" → Mode: Hosted Chat. Response Mode is under Options; pick Streaming. "Load Previous Session" → From Memory reads what the memory node stored for this session id, so a reloaded tab shows its history instead of a blank window.

    2. Memory. Change the memory to a persistent option: Postgres Chat Memory or Redis Chat Memory if your plan has a database. Otherwise keep the Window Buffer Memory, keyed on the session id, and write in your notes that a restart will empty it.

      Which key, and why it matters

      In the memory node, set Session ID to "Connected Chat Trigger Node". It then uses the id the widget sends with every message. The Window Buffer lives inside n8n's process; Postgres Chat Memory writes each turn as a row and survives restarts. If you add the redaction Set node from the snippets below, put it between the trigger and the agent.

    3. Welcome. Add a title, an opening message that says what the assistant is for, and three suggested prompts as buttons. Recognition over recall: nobody should face an empty box.

      If you cannot find suggested prompts

      Title, Subtitle and Initial Messages are in the Chat Trigger's Options. If your n8n version has no suggested-prompts setting, put the three prompts in the opening message as a numbered list, and add real buttons to your own page in Lab 5.2 with the widget's initialMessages option.

    4. Handover and identity. Paste the <handover> and <identity> sections you wrote on Day 3 into the agent's system prompt. Add an IF node after the agent that checks the amount against your threshold and sends a Gmail alert with the handover packet when it fires.

      Getting a flag out of the agent

      Ask the agent, in the prompt, to end every reply with a single line META: {"handover": false, "level": "read", "amount": 0}, and parse that line in a Code or Set node. Week 7's structured output parser does the same more tidily if you already have it wired.

    5. Two-browser test. Open the hosted chat in a normal window and in a private window at the same time, and work through the four checks below. Browser A must never see browser B's name.

    6. Record it. A two-minute screen recording of the two-browser test: both windows, the leak check and the execution log with the two session ids.

    Two-browser test checklist

    • Browser A: "My name is Priya Nair, from Stride." The agent greets Priya.

    • Browser B: "What is my name?" The agent must say it does not know yet.

    • Browser A, after a reload: "What is my name?" The agent must answer Priya, and the earlier turns must reappear.

    Check yourself

    Eight quick questions

    Pick an answer to see why it is right or wrong. Score 80 percent or more to pass. Nobody is watching; the point is to find out what did not stick.

    Think it through

    Why collect an email address with a scripted block instead of letting the agent ask for it?

    A scripted block checks the format and loops until it sees an "@" and a domain, then stores the result in a variable every later step can trust. The agent might accept a typo, forget to ask, or wander off topic, and you would only find out when the confirmation email bounced. A predictable step with a checkable answer belongs in a block. The MIT workbook's heuristic for this is error prevention: stop the mistake before it happens rather than apologising afterwards.

    What goes wrong if two users share the same session id?

    They share one memory. The agent mixes their names, orders and earlier messages, so user B can read what user A said, which is a privacy leak and a wrong answer in the same move. Nothing crashes, so nobody notices until a customer does. One session id per person is the rule, and on WhatsApp that means one per phone number; on a portal, one per signed-in account.

    Why restrict allowed origins on the chat webhook, and what does the restriction not do?

    The webhook is a public URL. Without an allowed-origins list, any website could embed your chat, spend your Claude budget and speak in your company's name. With the list, browsers refuse requests from pages on other hosts before the agent runs. It does not stop a script or a command line, which can send any header they like, so you still need a rate limit, a spending cap in the Console and a system prompt that declines off-topic use.

    A request to change the refund bank account arrives on WhatsApp from a number already on file. Why is the known number not enough?

    The number proves which phone sent the message, not who is holding it. Phones are lost, SIMs are swapped and staff leave with the company handset; and the MIT module on deepfakes is a reminder that the content of a message, even a voice note, is cheap to fake. Money moving is the top rung of the identity ladder: send a code to a contact you already trust, and have a person approve before anything changes. The assistant prepares; it does not execute.

    The MIT course says a one-size-fits-all interface fails because every user is different. What in your chat design adapts to the user, and what stays fixed?

    What adapts is the conversation: the agent reads the person's intention in their own words, handles two needs at once, remembers their name and open orders, and changes tone and length by channel. What stays fixed are the guardrails: the validated facts it must collect, the identity ladder, the handover triggers and the money threshold in an IF node. Adapt the experience, never the rules. That split is also what makes the thing testable, because the fixed part can be checked the same way every time.
