You wrote the instructions. Who decided what else is on the desk?

Think about the policy helper you built in Week 3. You wrote its instructions with care: a role, a task, rules, the XML tags from Week 2. That was prompt engineering, and it is still the right first move. But look at what the model actually sees on the eighth turn of a real conversation. Your 2,000 tokens of instructions are there. So are 6,000 tokens of policy sheets, the 48,000-token vendor contract the user dropped in at turn three, seven earlier exchanges, and the new question. The instructions you polished are about three percent of the desk.

Deciding what the other 97 percent should be is what the field now calls context engineering: choosing what the model sees, in what order, and for how long. It covers three things prompt engineering never had to. Dynamic state: the conversation and the user's documents, which change on every call. Tools: a web search or a parsed PDF comes back as text that lands on the desk whether you wanted all of it or not. And long-horizon assembly: an agent at step 30 of 40 is working from a desk that nobody wrote. It accumulated.

Switch the view on the left between the first turn, the eighth turn and the agent at step 30. The instructions never grow. Everything else does. In Week 1 I called the context window the model's desk; this week the question is who tidies it. For a single question, nobody needs to. For a 40-step agent, it has to be you, in the design, because a badly packed desk makes a perfect prompt fail.

Where the desk metaphor breaks. A person at a cluttered desk can ignore the clutter. The model cannot. Every token on the desk is read on every call, costs money on every call, and competes for attention with the one page that matters. That is the whole reason this week exists.

Prompt engineering writes the instructions. Context engineering decides everything else the model sees: what, in what order, and for how long.

Three tools, and what each one leaves on the desk

You already use three helpers in claude.ai without thinking of them as tools. You switch on web search when you need this quarter's numbers. You drop in a PDF and Claude reads it. You ask for a total and it writes a small program to work it out. The syllabus calls these Claude's native tools: web search, document parsing and the analysis sandbox. Each one is the model asking something outside itself, waiting, and getting text back.

That last part is what I want you to notice. The result comes back as text, and it lands in the context window like anything else. A web search returns a handful of pages, perhaps 3,000 to 4,000 tokens, with the sources Claude cites. A parsed 60-page contract becomes about 45,000 tokens and stays for the rest of the conversation. The sandbox is the frugal one: a dozen lines of code and a short printout, under a hundred tokens, and that printout is calculated rather than predicted. Press the three buttons on the left and watch each result take its place on the desk. Watch the total, too.

The sandbox fixes the arithmetic slips you collected in Week 1. The bare model, predicting digits, offers 372,781,809 for 48,219 times 7,731, which looks right and is not. The sandbox runs print(48219 * 7731) and the result is exact, because a program did the arithmetic and the model only wrote the program. Use it for any number you will act on: a total, a percentage, a date difference.

An honest note on sizes. The token counts here are typical, not exact. A search that returns long pages can cost more than a short PDF, and a parsed spreadsheet full of reference codes can be far larger than its page count suggests. The habit to build is to ask what a tool result will weigh before you let an agent call that tool fifty times.

A tool result is just more context, and it costs tokens on every later call. For numbers, let the sandbox calculate instead of the model guessing.

Day 2 · about 45 minutes

Where in the window a fact sits

Today is about position. The same fact is found more often at the start or the end of the window than in the middle, and less often as the window fills with pages that do not matter. By the end you can predict where the policy helper will miss, and you will have tested it with three documents of your own.

Hide one clause in a 60-page contract and ask for it

Here is a test you can run on any assistant. Take a 60-page vendor contract, pick one clause that matters, say the one that caps liability at twelve months' fees, and ask for it. Then move the clause to a different page and ask again. Researchers run a stricter version: they plant one odd sentence inside a huge document and ask the model to repeat it. The field calls it the needle-in-a-haystack test, and it is how long context windows get measured.

Current models mostly pass it. The interesting part is where they fail. Recall is highest when the needle sits near the start or near the end of the context and lowest when it sits in the middle. It drops again when there are several needles to find rather than one, because now the model has to hold five places at once. And it drops as the haystack grows, which is the next concept.

Play with the contract on the left. Hide the clause on page 3 and run twenty trials, then hide it on page 31 and run again. Then ask for five clauses instead of one. The chart fills in by position, and the shape it makes is the shape you will see in published tests. Two rules follow for everything you build. Put the material that matters most at the start or the end. And put your question after the documents, not before, so it is the freshest thing in the window when the model starts to answer.

The simulation is illustrative. The hit rates follow a curve shaped like published results; they do not come from a live model, and real numbers vary by model and by how different the needle is from its surroundings. A clause that reads like every other clause is harder to find than a sentence about pizza in a contract.

Position changes recall. Start and end are strong, the middle is weak, and more needles make it worse. Important material first or last; the question last.

Lost in the middle, and lost in the crowd

Picture how you read a long board pack the night before a meeting. The first pages get your full attention, the last page gets read because it holds the ask, and the forty pages between get skimmed. Language models show the same pattern, and a 2023 study gave it the name everyone now uses: lost in the middle. Attention is a U-shape across the window, strong at both ends and shallow in the centre. That is the mechanism behind the needle results, and behind the failure you may have caught in Week 1, when a detail from the middle of a long policy went missing.

There is a second effect, and it is the one that costs companies money. More context is not better context. Every page that does not matter competes for attention with the one that does, and the competition is not free. Drag the slider on the left to add irrelevant pages to a window that already holds the four documents an answer needs. Accuracy slides from the mid nineties towards seventy. Cost climbs in a straight line, because you pay for every token. The time to the first word climbs too, because every token has to be read before the answer can begin.

The marker at 100,000 tokens is a rule of thumb, not a law. Agents that never tidy their context tend to start making mistakes somewhere around there, long before the window is technically full. Treat it as the point where you should already have done something about the pile. Tomorrow we do that something.

Models improve at this every year. The newest ones hold up far better in the middle than the ones in the 2023 study. I still teach it, because the effect has shrunk rather than vanished, and because the cost and latency lines do not improve with model quality at all. You pay for every token whether the model attends to it or not.

Attention is a U-shape across the window, and every irrelevant page competes with the relevant one. More context costs more, takes longer, and past a point the answers get worse.

Packing the window, and emptying it again

Pack a briefing, not a filing cabinet

When a CFO asks how much notice the company must give to leave a facilities contract, you do not hand over the whole 60-page agreement, last quarter's support tickets and the board pack. You bring the termination clause, a line about who approves an early-exit fee, and the question. That is a briefing pack. A filing cabinet is what most first assistants are given instead: every document the team could find, uploaded once, present on every call.

Context packing is building the briefing pack on purpose, and it has three rules. Include only what this step needs, which usually means a section rather than a document. Put a one-line label in front of each document saying what it is, where it came from and what it is for, because otherwise the model has to work that out from the text, and a labelled document is found more reliably than a bare one. And order it: instructions first, documents next, the question last. The hero animation at the top of the page shows why that order also decides the bill, and Day 4 explains it.

Build the pack on the left for the CFO's question and check it. The verdict shows the tokens, an estimated accuracy and what answering that question a thousand times would cost at the current Sonnet price. Then press "Tick everything" and check again. The difference is not a rounding error.

A caveat on "only what the step needs". You do not always know in advance which section answers the question. Pruning: taking things off the desk when a step is done

Watch what happens on a long task. An agent is asked to email a customer the shipping rule that applies to order #4471. At step 4 it pulls a full CSV export, 42,000 tokens, to find the order. At step 9 it searches the web and gets ten pages of carrier rules. At step 10 it writes the one line it actually needed. Everything it read is still on the desk at step 30, and it will still be there at step 33 when the email goes out. Nobody put it there on purpose. It accumulated.

Document pruning is the habit of removing what a finished step no longer needs: the raw export once the two rows that matter have been copied out, the ten search pages once the summary exists, the error log once the error is fixed, the first draft once the second one replaces it. Press play on the left and compare the two modes. Without pruning the context crosses 100,000 tokens at step 31. With it, the agent sends the email from a desk of under 10,000 tokens, and every one of those tokens is something the next step needs.

In claude.ai you cannot really delete anything from a conversation, which is why today's exercise asks you to tell Claude to keep a summary and set the article aside. That is pruning by instruction, and it works less well than the real thing. In an agent built in n8n, from Week 7, you decide at each step what goes into the next call, and pruning becomes a design decision you can draw on a whiteboard. The API also offers automatic versions that clear old tool results or summarise the early conversation for you; we will meet them in Week 8.

Prune when a step is done: raw tool results after you have the summary, scratch work after the sub-task, old drafts after the new one. Keep the instructions, the goal and the latest results.

Day 4 · about 60 minutes

Caching: paying once for what never changes

Today is about the bill and the clock. Most of what your assistant sends on every call never changes, and the API can be told to read it once and reuse it. By the end you can predict the saving for your own product, read the four token counters in the Console, and you will have a Console account ready for tomorrow's lab.

Call 1 wrote the cache at 09:02:11. Send call 2 and watch the comparison run from the first token.

The cache stops at the first thing that changes

Think about what your policy helper sends on every single call: 3,000 tokens of instructions, 9,000 tokens of policy sheets, the 48,000-token vendor contract the team uploaded, and then one new question of a few hundred tokens. On the API you pay to have all 60,300 tokens read every time, and you wait while they are read. Yet 60,000 of them were identical to the previous call, a minute ago.

Prompt caching is the API's answer. You mark a point in the prompt with cache_control and the API stores everything up to that point. On the next call it compares the new prompt with the stored one from the very first token forward, and reuses the stored work for as long as the two match. The match stops at the first token that differs. That one sentence explains every rule that follows. Put the question before the documents and the first token already differs, so nothing behind it is reused. Put it last and the whole stable block in front of it is.

Press "Send call 2" on the left and watch the comparison run. Then switch the question to the front and run it again. Three facts to carry with you. A cached read costs about a tenth of the normal price. The first call, the one that writes the cache, costs about a quarter more. And the stored entry lives for five minutes from the last time it was read, so steady traffic keeps it alive and a quiet afternoon lets it expire. There is also a minimum size, a few hundred tokens on current models; a prompt smaller than that is silently not cached at all.

Prices and limits here are the published figures for claude-sonnet-5-5 as I write: $2 per million input tokens, cache writes at 1.25 times that, reads at a tenth, a 512-token minimum, a five-minute lifetime with a one-hour option at double the write price. Model names and prices change; the mechanism does not. Check the pricing page before you quote a number to a finance team.

The cache is a prefix match. Stable first, changing last; the match ends at the first token that differs. Reads cost a tenth, the first write costs a quarter more, and an entry lives five minutes from its last use.

What caching saves, in money and in seconds

Here is the sum a product manager does before switching caching on. Your assistant has a 60,000-token static block and answers 300 questions a day, each with 400 tokens of new material. Without caching that is 300 times 60,400 tokens at the input price. With it, the static block is written once and read 299 times at a tenth of the price, and only the 400 changing tokens are paid in full each time. Change the numbers on the left to your own. The saving sits around 85 to 90 percent whenever the static block is large and the calls are frequent.

Two switches show when caching does nothing, or worse. Put the question before the documents and the saving goes to zero, because the prefix breaks at the first token. Spread the calls more than five minutes apart and every call becomes a fresh write at a quarter above the normal price: caching now costs you money. That is the honest answer to "when would you not cache": a tiny static block, rare calls, or a "static" block that actually changes on most calls, for example a different document set for every user.

The clock matters as much as the bill. Reading 60,000 tokens takes time before the first word of the answer appears, and a cached read skips most of that work. Users feel it. For a support assistant answering live chats, the faster first word is often the reason the team notices caching at all; the cost saving is what finance notices a month later.

Model choice sits next to this decision. claude-haiku-5-5 costs a twentieth of claude-sonnet-5-5 per input token and suits high-volume steps such as sorting tickets; claude-opus-5-5 costs twice as much and suits the one step where quality matters most. Caching works on all three, and the largest savings come from a cheap model plus a cached block on the busy steps. Names change; check the current list.

Caching pays when a large stable block is reused within minutes. It saves nothing with the question first, and it costs extra when the calls are rare.

    Reading the Workbench: four token counters

    Tomorrow you will run the caching test for real in the Anthropic Console, the developer side of Claude where you talk to the API directly and see what each call costs. The Workbench is its prompt editor, and the part that matters today is the usage readout under every response. Four counters: input_tokens, cache_creation_input_tokens, cache_read_input_tokens and output_tokens. Learn to read them and the lab becomes easy.

    input_tokens is what was read at full price. cache_creation_input_tokens is what was written to the cache on this call, charged at a quarter more. cache_read_input_tokens is what was served from the cache, at a tenth. The three add up to the whole prompt; input_tokens alone is only the uncached remainder, which trips up almost everyone the first time. output_tokens is the reply, which caching does not touch.

    Press the buttons on the left in order. The first send writes the cache. The second, with the same question, reads it. Changing only the question still reads it, because the question comes last. Editing one word of the instructions forces a new write, because the prefix changed. Waiting six minutes forces a write too, because the entry expired. And shrinking the static block below the minimum caches nothing at all. If you ever see cache_read_input_tokens at zero on a call you expected to be cached, one of those things happened.

    The three input counters add up to the prompt. Reads at a tenth, writes at a quarter more, full price for the rest. A zero where you expected a read means the prefix changed, the entry expired, or the block was too small.

    Eight quick questions

    Think it through

    Why does putting the user's question before the documents break caching?

    The cache stores a prefix. On each call the API compares the new prompt with the stored one from the very first token and reuses the stored work only for as long as they match. If the question comes first, the first tokens differ on every call, so the match fails at once and every document behind the question is read again at full price. Put the question last and the whole stable block in front of it can be reused.

    An agent has run 30 steps and the context is 150,000 tokens. Name two things you would prune.

    Good candidates: a raw tool result, such as a full file export or a long search result, that a later step has already summarised; working notes from a sub-task that is finished; a draft that a newer draft has replaced; an error log for an error that was fixed. Keep the instructions, the user's goal, the current plan and the latest results. The rule is to remove what no future step needs, not what is merely old.

    When would you deliberately not use caching?

    When the stable block is tiny, because there is almost nothing to save and anything under the model's minimum is not cached at all. When calls are rare, because the entry expires five minutes after its last use and each fresh write costs a quarter more than a plain read. And when the "stable" content actually changes on most calls, for example a different document set for every user, because then every call is a write and never a read.

    Your assistant reads a 60-page contract for every question in a chat. Which parts of that call are static, which are dynamic, and where does the cache point go?

    Static: the instructions and any reference sheets, and, within one chat, the contract itself, since it does not change between questions. Dynamic: the conversation so far and each new question. The cache point goes at the end of the contract, so that instructions, sheets and contract are read once and reused, while the growing conversation and the new question sit after it. If a different user brings a different contract, the contract becomes dynamic across users and the cache point moves back to the end of the sheets.
